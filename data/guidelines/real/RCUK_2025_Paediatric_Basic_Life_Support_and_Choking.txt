Paediatric Basic Life Support and Choking (lay/bystander sections)

Source: European Resuscitation Council Guidelines 2025 Paediatric Life Support.
Resuscitation 2025;215 (Suppl 1):110767. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2025.110767
Published 27 October 2025.

Note: extracted from the RCUK "Paediatric Life Support (basic and advanced)"
guideline. Only the sections relevant to untrained/lay bystanders are included
here; the clinical PALS algorithm (drug dosing, defibrillation joules, ECPR,
hospital shock/seizure management) is deliberately excluded as out of scope

What is a paediatric patient

Paediatric patients are aged 0-18. "Infant" = neonates up to 1 year. "Child" =
1-12 years. "Adolescent" = 13-18 years.

Recognising a critically ill child (BBB-tool, for parents/caregivers)

All parents/caregivers should learn basic recognition of critical illness and
basic first-aid life-saving procedures. Immediately call for medical help or
call the ambulance service if a child shows signs of critical illness, described
by the BBB-tool (Behaviour, Breathing, Body colour):

Behaviour: a child who
- is not fully conscious or is difficult to rouse, floppy or rigid
- is having a seizure
- is confused, agitated, or interacting abnormally with parents/caregivers
- is crying inconsolably
- is not able to move one or more limbs
- has severe pain or is unable to speak or walk, if previously able to do so

Breathing: a child who has difficulty breathing, such that they
- are unable to take a deep breath
- are working hard to take each breath (breathing fast, grunting, flaring of
  the nostrils, indrawing between or under the ribs)
- are making additional noises while breathing
- are breathing too fast, too slowly or irregularly, or stop breathing
- adopt an abnormal posture to aid breathing

Body colour: the child's skin is cyanosed, mottled, abnormally pale or
greyish. Look at the hand palms, soles of the feet and mucosal membranes,
especially in children with dark skin tones.

Parents of children with specific chronic conditions should have an emergency
plan for any sudden deterioration, and caregivers should be trained in initial
life-saving procedures.

Paediatric basic life support (PBLS) — untrained rescuers and dispatcher-assisted CPR

If you encounter a child who appears unresponsive and you have no training
in PBLS, ensure your own safety and that of the child and follow the 3 steps
to save a life:
1. Check — see if the child reacts to a non-painful stimulus.
2. Call — call the ambulance service immediately if the child does not react,
   and follow the dispatcher's advice.
3. CPR — start CPR immediately following the instructions of the dispatcher.

Ambulance service call handlers should encourage bystanders to perform both
rescue breaths and chest compressions in children of all ages, using a 30:2
ratio with 5 initial rescue breaths for untrained bystanders or bystanders
trained only in adult BLS. If bystanders are not willing or able to perform
rescue breathing, dispatchers should encourage compression-only CPR in all
children. Call handlers should instruct bystanders to use age-specific
techniques for chest compressions and breathing in infants, children and
adolescents.

PBLS steps for trained rescuers in paediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest

- Ensure your own safety and that of the child.
- Use verbal and tactile stimulation to assess responsiveness. Do not use
  painful stimuli.
- Call, or have someone call, the ambulance service immediately, using the
  speaker function of your mobile phone. Follow the dispatcher's advice.
- Use the head tilt chin lift manoeuvre to open the airway, assess breathing
  and look for signs of life for no longer than 10 seconds.
- Give five initial rescue breaths.
- Immediately proceed with chest compressions.
- Continue CPR. Use a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 15:2 if you are
  specifically trained in PBLS; otherwise, use a ratio of 30:2.
- Focus on consistently high-quality compressions and effective
  ventilations. Minimise chest compression pauses.
- If a second rescuer is available, they should call the ambulance service
  while the first rescuer starts CPR, then bring and attach an AED as soon
  as possible for children of all ages. Once attached, follow the AED
  instructions.
- If there is only a single rescuer, calling the ambulance service and
  starting CPR should be prioritised over fetching and attaching an AED.
- Do not interrupt CPR unless there are clear signs of life, or you are
  instructed to do so by the AED.
- In an unresponsive child who is clearly breathing effectively, keep the
  airway open by continued head tilt/chin lift, or place them in the
  recovery position (not in suspected trauma), especially if there is a
  risk of vomiting. Check breathing continuously or at least every minute.

Airway and rescue breaths (no equipment)

- In infants, keep the head in a neutral position by slightly tilting the
  head and lifting the chin with two fingers on the chin bone (head tilt
  chin lift), without pressing on the soft tissues. In older children, more
  head tilt is needed. In adolescents, full extension of the head is needed,
  as in adults.
- Look for chest movement, listen and feel for airflow from the nose/mouth.
  If in doubt whether breathing is normal, act as if it is not normal.
- Blow steadily into the child's mouth (or infant's mouth and nose) for
  about 1 second, enough to make the chest visibly rise, then let it fall
  back passively before the next breath.
- If the chest does not rise, the airway may be obstructed: remove any
  visible obstruction in the mouth if easy to do so (do not perform a blind
  finger sweep), and reposition the head/chin.

Chest compressions

- Perform on a firm surface if immediately available. Remove clothes only
  if they hinder compressions.
- Compress the lower half of the sternum (breastbone) in all age groups.
- Infants: use the two-thumb encircling method.
- Children over 1 year: use the one-hand or two-hand technique (whichever
  achieves high-quality compressions).
- Rate 100-120 per minute. Depress the chest by at least one third of its
  depth; use the adult depth of 5-6 cm in adolescents, never exceeding 6 cm
  at any age. Allow full chest recoil between compressions. Do not interrupt
  compressions except for ventilations or AED instruction.

Using an AED on a child

- Follow the AED's instructions. Apply pads with minimal interruption to
  CPR (one person applies pads, a second continues CPR).
- Activate paediatric mode if available for infants and children under
  ~25 kg (roughly under 8 years old). For larger children/adolescents, or if
  no paediatric mode exists, use standard adult mode.
- Pad placement: for infants and children under 25 kg, use the
  anteroposterior position (one pad mid-chest left of the sternum, the
  other on the back between the shoulder blades). For children over 25 kg
  and adolescents, use the anterolateral position (below right clavicle,
  and left axilla) or the anteroposterior position; avoid placing pads over
  breast tissue in adolescents.
- Do not touch the child while the AED analyses the rhythm. Restart chest
  compressions immediately after a shock is delivered.

Foreign body airway obstruction (choking) — infants, children, adolescents

Suspect choking due to a foreign body if the child is unable to speak
(children/adolescents) or cry aloud (infants/smaller children), especially
during feeding, eating, or playing unsupervised.

- Call, or have someone call, the ambulance service as soon as possible.
- Encourage an older child or adolescent to cough.
- If coughing is not possible or becomes ineffective, give up to 5 back
  blows:
  - Infants: turn the infant face down along your forearm, forearm resting
    on your leg, supporting the head with your hand, head held lower than
    the thorax. Give a sharp blow between the shoulder blades. Repeat up
    to 5 times or until the obstruction clears.
  - Children/adolescents: lean them forward and give blows between the
    shoulder blades, up to 5 times.
- If back blows are not effective, give up to 5 chest/abdominal thrusts:
  - Infants: turn the infant onto their back on your knees. Use the
    two-thumb encircling technique to give sharper chest thrusts than
    for compressions. Repeat up to 5 times or until clear.
  - Children/adolescents: stand behind the child, arms around the upper
    abdomen, lean them forward, clench your fist between the navel and
    the breastbone, grasp it with your other hand, and pull sharply
    inwards and upwards. Repeat up to 5 times or until clear.
- If the child is still conscious and the obstruction persists, keep
  alternating up to 5 back blows with up to 5 chest/abdominal thrusts.
- Stop immediately if there are signs the obstruction has cleared
  (coughing, loud breathing, crying).
- Do not use blind finger sweeps in the mouth — only remove a clearly
  visible obstruction with a single sweep.
- Call for help/the ambulance service as soon as practical if not already
  done, at the latest when the child loses consciousness.
- Start CPR immediately with five rescue breaths as soon as the child
  becomes unconscious.
- RCUK/ERC make no recommendation for or against suction-based
  anti-choking devices, due to insufficient evidence.
