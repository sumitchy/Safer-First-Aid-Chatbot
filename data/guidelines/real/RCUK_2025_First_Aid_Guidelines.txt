Resuscitation Council UK
First Aid Guidelines 2025

Source: European Resuscitation Council Guidelines 2025 First Aid.
Resuscitation 2025;215 (Suppl 1):110752. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2025.110752
Published 27 October 2025.

Key points

Expectations of a first aid provider: Always call for help early and, ideally,
use a speakerphone, especially if alone. As a general principle, only use
equipment and medications you have been trained to use.
Structured assessment: Use an ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing,
Circulation, Disability, Exposure) to promptly identify and treat
life-threatening conditions.
Foreign body airway obstruction: Use an escalating approach — cough,
back blows, abdominal thrusts — in adults who are choking.
Life-threatening bleeding: an escalating approach with manual direct
pressure and, thereafter, haemostatic dressing and/or tourniquets.
Drowning: offer flotation, remove the person from the water, provide
immediate care as needed, including preventing hypothermia.

Introduction

First aid is a helping behaviour by anyone, for any emergency condition, in
any situation, including self-care. It typically involves recognising,
assessing and prioritising needs, providing care within the provider's
competencies while recognising their limitations, and seeking additional
help such as activating the emergency services. First aid serves as the
crucial first link in the chain of survival.

Expectations of a first aid provider

Three key principles minimise further injury, improve health and prevent
death:
1. Check for scene safety.
2. Call 999.
3. Only use available equipment or medications you have been trained to use.

There are occasions when a first aider may be directed by the emergency
services to use equipment they have not had in-depth training on.

First aid kits

All workplaces, leisure centres, public buildings, homes and cars should
have first aid kits. Public kits should be clearly marked and readily
accessible. Contents should be based on the setting, expected risks and the
users. Inspect and maintain kits regularly.

Cardiac arrest

If you suspect cardiac arrest, call 999 and follow the call handler's
instructions on performing CPR. Saving a life is the priority — do not be
concerned about causing harm. Continue CPR until professional help arrives
and takes over (or tells you to stop), the person becomes responsive
(speaks, opens eyes, moves purposefully, or breathes normally), or you
become exhausted.

Structured first aid assessment (ABCDE)

Pay immediate attention to safety, the person's responsiveness, and
life-threatening bleeding. Use the ABCDE framework (Airway, Breathing,
Circulation, Disability, Exposure) to structure your assessment.

Recovery position

Place adults and children with a decreased level of responsiveness who do
NOT meet the criteria for CPR into a lateral (side-lying) recovery position.
In cases of agonal breathing or trauma, do NOT move the person into the
recovery position.

Oxygen and pulse oximetry for acute breathing difficulty

If trained to deliver oxygen, give it to a person with difficulty breathing
and/or looking cyanosed (blue tinges on skin/lips; for darker skin tones,
look for pallor or blue tinges inside the lips). Use a pulse oximeter to
titrate, aiming for 94-98% saturation via simple face mask or non-rebreathing
mask (88-92% for known COPD). In life-threatening hypoxaemia
(saturation < 88%) out of hospital, give higher-flow oxygen to everyone,
including those with COPD.

Medical emergencies

Anaphylaxis

Suspect anaphylaxis if someone has:
- Breathing difficulties ranging from noisy breathing (stridor) from upper
  airway swelling to wheezing from lower airway obstruction.
- Flushing, rash (hives), cold or clammy skin, or feeling faint.
- Abdominal pain, vomiting, or diarrhoea.
- Recent exposure to a known food allergen or insect sting.

Actions:
- Call 999.
- Lie the person down unless breathing difficulties make sitting up (legs
  extended) preferable.
- Remove the trigger if known and possible.
- Give intramuscular adrenaline (self-administered or by a trained person)
  as soon as possible via autoinjector into the outer thigh.
- If symptoms persist 5 minutes after administration, give a second dose,
  ideally in the opposite leg.

Choking in an adult

Suspect choking if a person suddenly cannot speak or cough, particularly
while eating.
- Ask: "Are you choking?"
- Encourage coughing.
- If coughing is ineffective or impossible, give up to 5 back blows.
- If back blows are ineffective, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts.
- If choking is not relieved after 5 abdominal thrusts, call 999 and
  continue alternating 5 back blows with 5 abdominal thrusts until relieved
  or the person becomes unresponsive.
- Do NOT use blind finger sweeps to try to remove a foreign body.
- If the person becomes unresponsive, start CPR and ensure 999 has been
  called.
- Anyone treated for choking with abdominal thrusts or chest compressions
  should be evaluated by a healthcare practitioner afterward, since
  complications and injuries can occur.

Asthma

If a person with asthma has breathing difficulties, help them use their own
reliever inhaler, with a spacer device if available.

Chest pain

- Reassure the person; sit or lie them in a comfortable position.
- Call 999.
- Help a person with cardiac-sounding chest pain self-administer 300 mg of
  aspirin (ideally chewable/dissolvable) while awaiting help — but not if
  they have a known aspirin allergy.
- Help a person with known angina self-administer their nitroglycerine spray
  or tablets.
- Stay with them until help arrives.

Hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar)

Suspect in someone with diabetes or chronic malnutrition who suddenly has
impaired responsiveness or a behavioural change.
- If awake and able to swallow, give glucose or dextrose tablets (15-20 g)
  by mouth.
- If feasible, measure capillary blood sugar and treat if low
  (< 4.0 mmol/L or 70 mg/dL); re-measure after treatment.
- If tablets aren't available, give other dietary sugars — a handful of
  sugary sweets, or 50-100 mL of fruit juice or a sugary drink.
- If oral glucose isn't available, give a glucose gel (partly held in the
  cheek, partly swallowed).
- Repeat oral glucose if symptoms persist and haven't improved after 15 min.
- For an uncooperative but NOT unresponsive child, consider half a teaspoon
  of table sugar (2.5 g) under the tongue.
- Call 999 if the person is or becomes unresponsive, or doesn't improve.
- Once recovered (5-10 min after sugar), encourage a light snack.
- In an unresponsive person, do NOT give oral sugar (aspiration risk) —
  call 999 and consider the recovery position instead.

Opiate/opioid poisoning

Suspect an overdose if the person: is breathing slowly; has irregular or
absent breathing; is extremely drowsy or unresponsive; has very small
pupils.
- Call 999.
- If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR.
- Give naloxone if you are trained to.
- Reassess using the ABCDE approach.
- Follow the packaging instructions on repeat dosing.
- Keep the person under observation until emergency help arrives.

Stroke

Use a stroke assessment scale such as FAST (Face, Arm, Speech, Time) to
speed up recognition, and call 999. Only give oxygen if trained and the
person shows signs of hypoxia (blue tinges to skin/lips, or for darker skin
tones, pallor or blue tinges inside the lips).

Suicidal thoughts

- If you think a person might harm themselves, ask if they're alright, how
  they feel, and why. Ask directly if they have suicidal thoughts.
- Assess risk: has the person communicated about or made plans to take
  their own life?
- Explore their thoughts and listen non-judgmentally.
- If they have made concrete threats or plans, tell them you're going to
  get help, and call 999.
- If those warning signs aren't present, listen non-judgmentally and
  encourage seeking professional or other support (e.g. GP, a suicide
  hotline such as Samaritans).

Trauma emergencies

Cervical spinal motion restriction

Suspect a cervical spine injury after a fall/dive from height, crush by
machinery/heavy object, or a road traffic or sporting accident.
- Minimise neck movement. If awake and alert, encourage the person to hold
  their own neck in a comfortable, stable position.
- Never force an uncooperative person into any position — this may worsen
  an injury.
- If unresponsive and lying on their back: kneel behind their head and
  immobilise head/neck in a neutral in-line position (head or trapezius
  squeeze technique). Airway opening always takes priority over
  immobilisation — use a jaw-thrust, which should maintain neck neutrality.
- If unresponsive and lying face down: check the airway is open, hold the
  neck stable; if you need to open the airway, get help to roll them as a
  unit onto their back, keeping the neck in line, then apply the head/
  trapezius squeeze.
- Only those with specialised training (lifeguard, mountain rescue) should
  consider spinal motion restriction devices/collars, per their protocols.

Control of life-threatening bleeding

- Call 999.
- Apply firm, direct manual pressure to the bleeding site.
- Apply a standard, or ideally haemostatic, dressing directly to the wound,
  then firm direct pressure — packing the dressing into the wound if the
  site requires it. Any clean material can be used if no dressing is
  available; the priority is stopping the bleeding.
- Once controlled, apply a pressure dressing.
- Apply a tourniquet as soon as possible for life-threatening limb bleeding
  not controlled by direct pressure:
  - Place it around the limb 5-7 cm above the injury, not over a joint.
  - Tighten until bleeding slows and stops — this may be painful.
  - Write down the time it was applied.
  - Do not release it — only a healthcare professional should do that.
  - A second tourniquet above the first may be needed in some cases.

Open chest wounds

- Call 999.
- Leave an open chest wound exposed to the air — do NOT apply a dressing or
  cover it.
- If necessary, control localised bleeding with direct pressure.
- If trained and equipped, apply a specialised non-occlusive/vented
  dressing that allows air to escape on exhalation. Watch for airflow
  obstruction from bleeding or clotted blood.

Preservation of an amputated body part

- Manage severe bleeding first.
- Retrieve the part quickly and keep it cold without freezing it:
  - Wrap it in a sterile dressing or clean cloth moistened with saline or
    water.
  - Place it in a clean, watertight bag or container.
  - Place that inside another bag/container with ice or ice water (or a
    cooler/instant cold packs if no ice).
  - Keep it cooled at all times; avoid direct contact with ice or freezing.
    Label the container with the person's name and the time it was stored.
  - Transport the part with the injured person to the same hospital as
    quickly as possible.

Concussion

Suspect concussion if a person has trouble thinking/remembering, physical
symptoms (headache, vision changes, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, seizures,
light/noise sensitivity), emotional changes, or behaviour changes (increased
sleepiness, reduced activity, loss of responsiveness, confusion).
- Remove them from physical activity.
- Refer to a healthcare professional for assessment and advice.

Environmental emergencies

Drowning

- If not trained in water rescue, do NOT enter the water — you risk
  drowning too.
- If the person is awake and responsive, stay on land and reach out with a
  flotation device, lifebuoy, rescue tube, or other equipment.
- Trained first aiders/lifeguards in the water or on a boat: call 999 before
  entering the water; provide a flotation device; keep the person's head
  out of the water; if unresponsive and not breathing, and it's feasible and
  safe with an effective flotation device, give five rescue breaths in the
  water as soon as possible; retrieve them to land/boat as soon as possible.
- Once on land/boat: check if unresponsive and not breathing. If so, give
  five rescue breaths immediately, then begin standard CPR.
- If an AED is available, dry the chest quickly and follow its prompts.
- Continue CPR and AED use until the person shows signs of life or
  emergency help arrives and takes over.

Prevention of hypothermia

- Insulation: cover with dry blankets/clothing to minimise heat loss.
- Wind protection: shield from wind with barriers or move to shelter.
- Wet clothing removal: gently remove wet clothing, replace with dry.
- Ground isolation: place insulating material between the person and cold
  ground.
- In settings where hypothermia is common, use tailored prevention plans
  and training.

Heat stroke

Suspect heat stroke if the person shows confusion, agitation, disorientation,
seizures or unresponsiveness — can occur in high ambient temperatures,
especially during endurance sporting events (exertional heat stroke can
occur at any ambient temperature during exertion).
- Remove from the heat source; start passive cooling by removing excess
  clothing and moving to a cooler/shaded spot.
- Use whatever active cooling is available immediately. Gold standard:
  whole-body (neck down) cold water (1-26°C) immersion until core
  temperature falls below 39°C.
- Alternatives: tarp-assisted cooling oscillation (TACO), ice sheets,
  commercial ice packs, a fan alone, cold shower, hand cooling devices,
  cooling vests/jackets, or evaporative cooling (mist + fan).
- Monitor core temperature if possible (rectal thermometer). If not
  possible, cool for 15 min or until neurological symptoms resolve,
  whichever comes first.
- Cool first, transfer second. Continue cooling during transport to a
  medical facility.

Snake bite

The only venomous snake native to the UK is the Adder (European Viper),
with haemolytic toxic venom.
- Call 999.
- Keep the person calm and at rest.
- Keep the bitten limb still and immobilised — this may slow venom spread.
- Remove tight clothes, rings or watches from the affected limb.
- Avoid harmful actions: do NOT apply a pressure dressing, ice, heat, or a
  tourniquet. Do NOT cut the wound or try to suck out venom.
